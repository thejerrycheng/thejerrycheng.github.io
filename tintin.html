TINTIN

Thrust-Integrated Neural Touchdown. A 5,000-tonne lunar lander with one gimbaled engine, 100 m long, losing a fifth of its mass on the way down. It has to fall two hundred metres, kill seventy metres of downrange offset and set down inside a ten-metre circle. Below you can fly it yourself in full 6-DoF, hand it to a cascaded 3-D controller, or hand it to a SAC policy at any point in its training — and re-run the Monte Carlo in your own browser.

The descent in MuJoCo: entry, the attitude capture that turns the engine into the velocity vector, and the terminal burn onto the pad. The vehicle is the rocket from The Adventures of Tintin, at 5,000 tonnes and 100 m; the terrain is an imported lunar mesh.

The task

Powered descent is usually split into three boxes: navigation estimates the state, guidance plans a trajectory, and a tracking controller flies it. The split is convenient and it is also where the problem hides — on a vehicle whose mass drops by 90 % during the burn, the tracking controller is chasing a plan made for a different vehicle.

TINTIN removes the split. One policy sees the state and emits throttle and two gimbal angles. That is Integrated Guidance and Control: what trajectory to fly and how to actuate are learned as one map.

The state, action and dynamics are the same in the report, in the MuJoCo environment, in the Python harness that made the plots below, and in the sandbox you are about to fly:

st = [ p, v, q, ω, m, I ]    ut = [ T, θp, θy ] ∈ [−1, 1]3
ṁ = −T / (Isp g0)     I(t) = I0 · m(t)/m0     T = Tmax(uT+1)/2,   θy,p = 30° uy,p

Mass depletion is not decoration. Burning 4,500 t out of 5,000 t changes both the acceleration a given throttle buys and the inertia a given gimbal angle has to rotate — the plant changes underneath the controller.

Fly it yourself

This is the actual vehicle: 5,000 tonnes dry, 1,000 tonnes of propellant, one 25 MN engine on a ±30° gimbal 30 m below the centre of mass, lunar gravity, six degrees of freedom. The model in your browser is a transliteration of the MuJoCo one — same inertia tensor, same RK4 at 100 Hz, same second-order gimbal servos — and it tracks MuJoCo to under 1.3 m over a full 45-second descent. Take the controls, hand them to the 3-D PD baseline, or hand them to the trained SAC policy at any point in its training.

Flying it with a classical controller

Before any learning, the vehicle needs a baseline, and the baseline has to fly the same thing the policy does — the real MuJoCo body, in three dimensions, with the gimbal and the mass loss. scripts/tools/pd3d.py is a cascaded controller: an outer loop turns position and velocity error into a desired thrust vector, and an inner loop drives the two gimbal angles to point the vehicle along it. Building it turned up four things about this vehicle that a textbook cascade gets wrong.

alat = kd(vref(d) − vxy)    vzref = −√(2 aup (h − hflare))    fdes = m(ades + g ez)
e = [0,0,1] × RT ûdes    τdes = I(m)(kpe − kdω)    θp = asin(−τy / L T),   θy = asin(−τx / L T cos θp)
1

You cannot tilt a 100-metre rocket near the ground

Bisecting the resting height of the MuJoCo mesh over a range of tilts gives a straight line: the centre of mass touches down at z = 1.00 + 0.2475 × tilt[°], R² > 0.999. The vehicle is 100 m long with a ~17 m fin radius, so at 20° of tilt a fin hits the ground with the centre of mass still 6 m up.

That makes the attitude and altitude channels physically coupled, and a cascade that treats them as independent flies a textbook approach straight into the pad at 16° and 4 m/s — which is exactly what the first version did, every time. The fix is a tilt cap that is a function of altitude: θ ≤ 4 (h − 1.5) degrees.

2

You cannot steer while falling

Lateral acceleration is (az + g) tan θ. If the vertical channel throttles back to a near-free-fall descent then az + g → 0, and the gimbal buys essentially no lateral authority at any tilt at all. The controller was commanding 35° of tilt and getting 0.2 m/s² out of it.

Holding at least hover thrust while there is still downrange to kill costs propellant and fixes it. It is the single change that took the easy case from 10 % to 70 %.

3

The authority is the tilt cap, not the thrust-to-weight

TWR 3 suggests 3·g·sin(35°) = 2.8 m/s² of lateral acceleration. The real number during an approach is g·tan(35°) = 1.13 m/s², because the vertical channel is only asking for about hover thrust and the tilt merely redirects it. Sizing the guidance for 2.0 m/s² instead of 1.1 made the vehicle command approach speeds it could not arrest, and it flew through the pad in a limit cycle it never left.

4

Braking laterally means reversing the whole vehicle

There is no lateral thruster: to stop translating, the entire 5,000-tonne body has to tilt the other way, which takes about 3 seconds during which the deceleration is zero. So the approach speed has to satisfy d = v²/2a + v·tlag, not the textbook v = √(2ad). At 10 m of remaining offset that is 2.7 m/s instead of 4.7 — and the difference between those two numbers is the difference between landing and skidding past the pad.

Learning it instead

The alternative is to stop designing the cascade and let one network map the state to throttle and both gimbal angles. SAC, on the same MuJoCo environment, with the initial-condition curriculum and a randomised vehicle every episode: dry mass ±15 %, propellant load ±30 %, specific impulse ±10 %, engine authority ±15 %, a permanent gimbal misalignment up to 2° per axis, a constant lateral disturbance, and noise on the position, velocity and attitude the policy sees.

Three things had to be fixed before it learned anything

Hovering paid better than landing. The first reward gave a +0.3 per-step "alive" bonus on top of a +4 upright term. Over a 4,500-step limit that made hovering to the time limit worth about 19,000 against 8,300 for a successful landing, and SAC duly learned to hover — success peaked at 20 % around 50 k steps and decayed to 6 % by 250 k. Every shaping term is now a penalty, so the only way to score is to finish, and to finish quickly.

Crashing tidily paid too. The partial credit for a hard landing was −300 + 250q with q ∈ [0, 3], so a crash that arrived near the pad, roughly upright, scored +450. Now it is −400 + 300(q/3) — still graded, always negative.

The update-to-data ratio was 0.025. With ten environments and train_freq=32, SB3 collects 320 environment steps per training call; with gradient_steps=8 that is one gradient step per forty samples. SAC had managed roughly six thousand gradient steps by 250 k environment steps. Raising it to 80 changed the whole curve.

None of these are exotic. All three are invisible unless you watch the training curve and ask why a sample-efficient algorithm is not being sample-efficient.

Reproduce it

Every number and figure on this page regenerates from scratch with fixed seeds, in the project repository.

# everything below runs against the same MuJoCo vehicle git clone https://github.com/thejerrycheng/space_robot_mujoco cd space_robot_mujoco pip install mujoco gymnasium stable-baselines3 torch matplotlib # the 3-D cascaded PD baseline, Monte Carlo over the evaluation envelope python scripts/tools/pd3d.py --trials 200 # train SAC with the wide domain randomisation, checkpointing at each stage OMP_NUM_THREADS=1 python scripts/tools/train_sac_dr.py --name sac_dr --steps 1500000 --envs 10 # evaluate every stage against the baseline, draw the figures, # and export each policy to JSON for the sandbox on this page python scripts/tools/eval_progress.py --run sac_dr --episodes 120 # cross-check the browser model against MuJoCo python scripts/tools/validate_js_model.py /tmp/mj_ref.json

The browser model in assets/js/tintin3d.js is a transliteration of the MuJoCo vehicle: same inertia tensor, same RK4 at 100 Hz, the same second-order gimbal servos (fitted from a 0.3 rad step response: ωn = 54.5 rad/s, ζ = 0.30), and the same measured tilt-dependent contact height. Replaying an identical action sequence through both, the two trajectories stay within 1.29 m over a full 45-second descent — under 0.5 % of path length — and the attitudes within 2.9×10⁻⁴ in |qw|. Modelling the gimbal servo rather than treating it as instantaneous is worth a factor of seven in that number. The outcome distributions agree less tightly, within roughly 15 percentage points, because a landing is decided in the last metre.
