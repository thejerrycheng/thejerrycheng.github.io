TINTIN

Thrust-Integrated Neural Touchdown. A 5,000-tonne lunar lander with one gimbaled engine has to fall 500 m, flip, kill 500 m of downrange offset and set down inside an 80 m circle — with the vehicle losing mass the whole way. Below you can fly it yourself, hand it to the report's PD autopilot, re-run the Monte Carlo in your own browser, and read what the learned SAC policy did instead.

The full descent in MuJoCo: entry, the flip that turns the engine into the velocity vector, and the terminal burn onto the green pad. The vehicle is the rocket from The Adventures of Tintin; the terrain is imported lunar mesh.

The task

Powered descent is usually split into three boxes: navigation estimates the state, guidance plans a trajectory, and a tracking controller flies it. The split is convenient and it is also where the problem hides — on a vehicle whose mass drops by 90 % during the burn, the tracking controller is chasing a plan made for a different vehicle.

TINTIN removes the split. One policy sees the state and emits throttle and two gimbal angles. That is Integrated Guidance and Control: what trajectory to fly and how to actuate are learned as one map.

The state, action and dynamics are the same in the report, in the MuJoCo environment, in the Python harness that made the plots below, and in the sandbox you are about to fly:

st = [ p, v, q, ω, m, I ] ∈ ℝ17    ut = [ T, θp, θy ]
ṁ = −T / (Isp g0)     I(t) = I0 · m(t)/m0     T = Tmax (uT+1)/2

Mass depletion is not decoration. Burning 4,500 t out of 5,000 t changes both the acceleration a given throttle buys and the inertia a given gimbal angle has to rotate — the plant changes underneath the controller.

Given

  • Full state (fully observable MDP)
  • Lunar gravity, engine limits
  • Target at (0, 0, 0)

Commanded

  • Throttle T ∈ [0, Tmax]
  • Gimbal pitch θp ∈ ±30°
  • Gimbal yaw θy ∈ ±30°

  • Touchdown (CoM < 55 m)
  • |x| > 700 m, ‖v‖ > 200 m/s

What a classical controller gets you

The report's baseline is a cascaded PD: an outer loop turns position and velocity error into a desired acceleration vector, that vector becomes a thrust magnitude and a desired tilt, and an inner loop drives the gimbal to hold that tilt. To put a number on it I re-ran it — 500 descents from the deployment ellipse, one seed, everything in scripts/tools/run_pid_baseline.py.

The propellant is not the problem

I expected mass depletion to be what breaks the fixed-gain controller, so I swept the propellant fraction from 30 % to 95 % of the wet mass. The success rate did not move — because the PD burns only 3.8 % of the load on a nominal descent, it never gets near the dry mass, and the plant it is tuned for is the plant it flies. Mass depletion is a real problem for this vehicle, but not one a 70 s lunar descent at TWR 3 exposes.

What does break it is the shape of the feasible set. A fixed gain pair defines one basin; the report's deployment ellipse is wider than that basin, and the entry conditions on its edge are exactly the ones the learned policy is trained to cover.

Learning it instead

The alternative is to stop designing the loop and let one network map the state to throttle and both gimbal angles. Three algorithms went in under an identical environment and reward — SAC, PPO and DQN over a discretised 3×3×3 action table — and only one came out with a controller.

The observation exploits the symmetry of the problem: approaching from any azimuth is the same problem, so the translational state collapses to lateral distance r = √(x²+y²) and altitude z. That drops the effective dimensionality without giving up any 6-DoF fidelity.

Three things made it train:

  • Attitude-gated shaping. The approach and descent terms only pay out once the vehicle is roughly upright. Without the gate the policy learns to dive at the target head-first — it is a shorter path and the distance term does not care which way up you arrive.
  • Curriculum. The success box starts generous and tightens once the policy clears 80 % at the current level. Landing radius, descent speed and tilt all shrink together.
  • Domain randomisation. Every episode resamples pose, velocity, tilt, yaw and mass over the whole deployment ellipse, so the policy never gets to specialise on one entry.

Global guidance is the easy half

Across 100 randomised entries the learned policy produces a consistent approach geometry and a near-vertical glideslope. The failures are not navigation failures — 43 % terminate on excessive tilt, 27 % on lateral drift, 19 % on terminal velocity and 11 % on timeout. Every one of those is a last-phase failure.

The same thing shows up in my PD re-run from the opposite direction: when the baseline lands it lands to 6.5 m and 0.9°, and when it fails it fails on the way in, not on the pad. Terminal attitude stabilisation under a shrinking mass and a shrinking time budget is the hard part of this problem, and neither a fixed-gain loop nor a single end-to-end policy solves it cleanly.

Reproduce it

Every number and figure on this page regenerates from scratch with fixed seeds. The harness is NumPy-only; the MuJoCo environment and the SAC training script live alongside it.

# the browser sandbox, the plots and the tables all come from this one model git clone https://github.com/thejerrycheng/space_robot_mujoco cd space_robot_mujoco # 500 PD descents + all five figures, vector PDF into docs/figures/ python scripts/tools/run_pid_baseline.py --trials 500 # the full 6-DoF MuJoCo environment and the SAC training run python train_gym_sac.py --num-envs 16 --total-steps 5000000 python test_gym_sac.py --model models/gym_sac_UPRIGHT_4/best_model

The browser model in assets/js/tintin_lander.js is a line-by-line transliteration of scripts/tools/rocketgym_planar.py; the two agree to within Monte Carlo noise (70.2 % vs 69.5 % over 500 and 200 descents respectively).
