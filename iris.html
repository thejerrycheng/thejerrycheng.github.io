IRIS

The Intelligent Robotic Imaging System: a six-axis cinema robot arm built for under a thousand dollars, and a visuomotor policy that learned camera moves from demonstrations. This page is the studio version of the thesis: the arm's real kinematic model bolted to a desk in your browser, a Sony α7R III with a motorised 16–150 mm zoom on its wrist, twenty-two shots from a pan to a dolly zoom to object tracking, hand control in joint and task space, and a data-collection and training loop you can run yourself. Every shot was planned against the arm's joint limits, speeds and motor torques, then recorded from the same simulator.

Showreel from the studio: the arm on the desk, the camera feed with its viewfinder, and the shots one after another. The framing you see is what the lens sees in the simulation, depth of field included.

In one strip

1 · Build

A cinema arm for the price of a lens

Six FOC motors, an offset-axis layout for unobstructed sweeps, belt transmissions that move the motors toward the base, and a body that prints in 23 parts. The thesis covers the sizing, the DH table and the iterations that broke.

2 · Fly

The same kinematics, on a desk, in a browser

The MuJoCo body tree of the thesis drives the arm; a damped-least-squares solver aims the lens; every preset is a parametric camera path plus a lens track, checked by the planner before it ever plays.

3 · Learn

Record, train, let it fly

The thesis learned camera moves from 17,897 demonstration samples with a compact temporal head. The studio lets you record your own episodes at 10 Hz, train a small action-chunking policy in the tab, and hand it the controls.

A motorised zoom, and the shots only a robot can do

The thesis flew an Intel RealSense. The next IRIS carries a real camera: a full-frame Sony α7R III (35.9 × 24 mm) and a 16–150 mm zoom whose zoom and focus rings are driven by servos on a bracket under the lens, so a shot can command focal length and focus distance as continuous tracks, slew-rate limited like any other axis. The simulator models the field of view, the barrel extension, the depth of field from the thin-lens circle of confusion, and the two effects that need the arm and the lens to move together.

field of view: 2·atan(24 / 2f) vertical  ·  circle of confusion: c = (f/N)·|d − S|/d · f/(S − f)  ·  dolly zoom, subject of radius r: f(t) = f₀ · √(d(t)² − r²) / √(d₀² − r²)  →  r = 0 gives the textbook f₀·d/d₀

Dolly zoom. The arm pulls the lens back along the optical axis while the zoom servo pushes in, holding the product's size in the frame; the room behind it collapses. The reverse, pushing in while zooming out, makes it rush away. Two details decide whether that reads on screen. The first: the size that has to stay fixed is the silhouette of a body with a radius, not a point at the subject distance — a sphere of radius r at distance d subtends a half-width r/√(d² − r²), so the focal track carries the same square root, and the textbook law leaves a few per cent of breathing on a subject this close. The second: the subject has to fit in the frame. The carrot cake is 225 mm across (measured in the scene, not the 98 mm the spec's height field lists), so the old 70 mm preset framed it at 1.5 frame widths — it ran off both sides and only a 2 mm slice of it was in focus. Both shots now run the arm from 0.26 m to 0.42 m along the optical axis with the cake at 65 % of the frame width, 24 → 42 mm at f/16.

The planner's coefficient of variation on the subject's width is — for the dolly zooms against — for a plain dolly over the same travel. As executed — servo lag, joint tracking and all — the telemetry gives 0.1 % for each dolly zoom. And segmenting the cake out of the rendered frames themselves, the width the audience sees holds inside a 2.4 % band across the shot, while the plain dolly's swings 38 %.

Rack focus. Only the focus servo moves: near product, far product, back, with the aperture open to f/2.8 so the pull is visible. Object tracking. A toy car laps the desk and the wrist keeps it centred, either from the car's true position or from a pixel tracker running on the feed itself, the same loop the studio's policy tab uses to read images.

From a camera path to six motors

The arm in the studio is the thesis's MuJoCo model, body for body: the fixed pose of each link in its parent, the hinge axis, the joint range. A forward-kinematics and Jacobian twin in Python is checked against MuJoCo to 10⁻⁹ m; the browser runs the same equations. The tool frame is the lens's entrance pupil, 14 cm ahead of the mount on the optical axis, so a shot's camera position means the lens, not the flange.

Each frame the solver takes the target lens pose (position and a look-at orientation with a level horizon) and runs damped least squares from the previous joint vector; when a pose is at the edge of reach it retries from eight canonical postures and keeps the closest. Joints 4 and 6 are continuous-rotation motors, so paths through ±180° are taken the short way round. A 1.5 rad/s per-joint cap keeps the motion cinematic; the whip pan is allowed 3 rad/s.

The planner samples every preset at 30 Hz, solves the IK along it and asks MuJoCo for the joint torques by inverse dynamics with the 1.5 kg camera payload. It found the shots the first drafts could not do: a low 360° orbit folds the wrist past its ±100° pitch, a straight-down top view sits on the look-at singularity, and any lens position closer than about 30 cm to the base column folds the arm onto itself. The library on this page is what survived, with the numbers in the table above.

The thesis, in short

1

Design

Four actuator families compared, torque sizing at the shoulder (25.8 N·m), elbow (10.6) and wrist (4.0), and the Unitree GO-M8010-6 chosen at $70 each. Offset-axis kinematics for full sweeps, GT2 belts to move the motors toward the base, a spherical wrist so IK splits into position and orientation. Repeatability ±5 mm, 60–100 W.

2

Control and simulation

MuJoCo model with the real meshes; inverse-dynamics tracking of circles and lines with the camera's optical axis pinned on a subject (the pan, dolly and crane presets of the thesis); numerical and analytic IK; a ROS Noetic stack over RS-485 at 200 Hz with encoder calibration and a real-to-sim visualiser.

3

Learning

VisionJointPlanner: a ResNet-18 feature per frame, five frames of RGB and joints and a goal point in, the next five joint states out, trained on 17,897 samples with an MSE plus continuity loss. Deployed at 30 Hz with a goal-directed start and a stagnation detector to beat the zero-action attractor; the policy generalised zero-shot across backgrounds and around obstacles.

Collect data and train a policy in the tab

The Record & train tab of the studio reproduces the thesis's loop at toy scale. Record saves an episode at 10 Hz: joint angles, focal length, focus distance and a 24 × 16 grey thumbnail of the feed, tagged with the shot and the product positions. Demo every preset records the whole library in one go at triple speed. Train fits a VisionJointPlanner-lite in TensorFlow.js: a five-frame window of states and the thumbnail through a tiny CNN, a goal token, a 256-wide MLP, and the next five joint and lens deltas out (action chunking); training takes seconds. Run hands the policy the arm with ACT-style temporal ensembling over the chunks, until it finishes or stalls.

It is honest about what it is: a few thousand samples and a few hundred thousand parameters, enough to reproduce a shot it has seen and to show the failure modes the thesis met at full scale, the zero-action attractor above all. Export the dataset as JSON to train something bigger offline.

node record_videos.js              # drive iris_studio.html headless (GPU), 30 fps -> mp4 + telemetry
python make_figures.py             # docs/figures/*.pdf, site PNGs, numbers
cd ../docs && make                 # iris_studio.pdf

The spec that both sides read is assets/data/iris_studio.json: the desk, the product sets, the car's track and the 22 shots. The Python package irisstudio (kinematics, shots) and the browser modules assets/js/iris/{kin,lens,shots,scene,dof,policy,app}.js implement the same rules, so what the planner validates is what the studio plays.

Assets. Arm links: the thesis's STL meshes, decimated to 1.7 MB. Products: CC0 models from Poly Haven (apple, bananas, lemon, lime, kiwi, avocado, pomegranate, pears, two cakes, wine bottles, a vase, a succulent, two watches) plus procedural props (a takeaway coffee cup, a perfume bottle, a lipstick, a cream jar, the toy car). Camera and lens are procedural, to scale.

Not modelled. Motor dynamics and belt compliance (the arm follows the planned joints under a speed cap), self-collision, the real lens's breathing and minimum-focus curve, and the camera's exposure. The report lists the limitations.
