Shared intent. Local action.

What must a team share beyond feedback through the object?

Copies of the same policy do not automatically form a coordinated team. When two robots hold one load, every action changes the other robot’s motion and support.

We study this boundary with a shared planner and learned local actors. Grasp-aware geometry constrains the route; each robot generates its own actions. The evaluated system uses a supervisor to coordinate acquisition and lifting.

Abstract

Cooperative transport couples geometric planning with contact acquisition, timing and load support. We present a grasp-aware planning and learned-control framework with explicit shared and local information. An assembly model accounts for both the payload and its carriers; an object-level model predictive controller supplies references to a shared learned policy. Aligning global and local planning geometry and margins raises a paired eight-scene result from 36/40 to 40/40. A separate frozen ten-scene evaluation completes 35/40 missions with randomized starts and activation delays up to eight seconds. These results establish a supervised simulation baseline for studying communication amount, message content and coordination through a shared object.

The research questions

Common weights are only the beginning.

RQ1

How much must be shared?

How much information does a mechanically coupled team need beyond local sensing and object feedback?

RQ2

What is worth communicating?

Task intent, grasp geometry, object motion and partner events convey different information. Which content matters at a fixed budget?

RQ3

Can task-level agreement suffice?

Can each robot work out its own actions when contact, load and partner behavior change?

The paper evaluates the planning and supervised-control baseline. Minimum communication and learned readiness are questions for controlled ablations, not established results.

Method

One shared planner. Two copies of the same policy.

The planner reasons about the whole assembly. Local actors turn common geometric references into base and upper-body actions.

01

Plan the grasped assembly

RRT-Connect searches with the object and both carrier footprints. Grasp offsets change the orientations that can fit through an aperture.

02

Track object motion

A 10 Hz object-level MPC follows measured path progress. A stall does not advance the reference simply because time passes.

03

Act locally

The same frozen actor runs at 50 Hz for each robot. Local observations and references produce 21 action outputs per robot.

Shared in the evaluated system

Assembly geometry, object motion references, and supervised phase/height decisions. Attachment state informs progression.

Local to each robot

Its observation, action inference and motor commands. Shared weights do not imply identical actions from different states.

Proposed extension

Shared task intent and timestamped object estimates; private recurrent memory and contact feedback. Replace readiness supervision through learning.

Results · frozen paper cohort

Where does coordination break down?

Forty missions start before grasp acquisition. The test covers ten scenes, 1–2 kg simulated loads, ±5% dimensions and independent activation delays of 0–8 s.

Grasps change what fits.

The illustrated beam’s admissible yaw narrows from 25.8° to 5.5° when the carrier footprints are included. Planning only for the payload misses this constraint.

Global and local geometry must agree.

Aligning the collision representation and margin raises the paired result from 36/40 to 40/40. This changes both geometry and margin; it does not isolate either effect.

A late partner need not prevent acquisition.

With a 7.68 s activation delay, attachment occurs at 1.88 and 10.76 s; transport begins at 13.44 s. A supervisor controls progression in this experiment.

Failures concentrate before transport.

All five failed missions stop before verified-route transport: two panel tips, a beam timeout, an incomplete office lift and a living-room search failure.

Development results and scope


The earlier declared test completes 46/48 full missions across twelve scenes, with 1–2 kg loads, ±5% dimensions, 0.08–0.50 m approach radii and activation delays up to eight seconds. It uses a gated controller and idealized welded retention. It does not establish recovery with a loose or dropped grasp.

One policy, local information
A two-layer causal transformer has 84,567 actor parameters and a separate 16-frame history per robot. It receives own encoders, grasp errors, local load/contact, relative object SE(3) pose, task geometry and measurement ages. Partner state and coordination-phase inputs are absent.
The planner chooses the pose
Desired object orientation and nearby path targets come from the high-level planner. The spatial reference follows measured progress. The actor commands base motion, lift/arm/torso rates and gripper closure targets. A zero lift rate holds position.
Contact and uncertainty
Finite, breakable contact attachments require physical rail contact and closure. Object, hand and odometry estimates include configurable noise, latency and dropout. A 2° base-tilt guard remains strict. Contact forces are an uncalibrated simulation approximation.


Open problems and research basis
Recovering from a mid-run release, arbitrary approach offsets, and learned large-angle object flips remain open. Regrasp alone is insufficient: a fixed-command diagnostic also reconnects but fails the full task. Memory and load-feedback ablations, broader scene tests and hardware calibration remain necessary.
The temporal policy follows the causal-transformer direction of Radosavovic et al. Decentralized shared-load coordination is informed by decPLM and decMBC. These precedents do not establish reliability for our robot or grasp mechanics.






Discussion
From shared references to shared understanding.
Count every information source
A robot can avoid partner telemetry while still receiving shared object estimates and motion commands. Communication budgets must account for both.
Separate intent from dynamics
A goal states what to accomplish. Grasp geometry constrains feasible motion. Object and contact feedback reveal how execution is unfolding.
Learn when to wait
Local memory and load feedback may reveal a stalled partner. The next test is whether those observations can replace supervised readiness under slip and changing support.
The current paper establishes a simulation baseline. Communication-budget ablations, autonomous readiness, reliable grasp-loss recovery and hardware validation remain open.


Research context
Related directions
Shared-policy collaboration connects to CHORUS and cross-embodiment learning in CrossFormer. Oregon State’s decMBC and decPLM study decentralized carrying under different contact assumptions. PAINT investigates partner-intent inference through local observations. The paper discusses these interfaces in more depth.
