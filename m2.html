Shared intent. Local action.

What must a team share beyond feedback through the object?

Copies of the same policy do not automatically form a coordinated team. When two robots hold one load, every action changes the other robot’s motion and support.

We study this boundary with a shared planner and learned local actors. Grasp-aware geometry constrains the route; each robot generates its own actions. The evaluated system uses a supervisor to coordinate acquisition and lifting.

01 / Method

Agree on object motion. Act from local feedback.

The planner reasons about the whole assembly. Local actors turn common geometric references into base and upper-body actions.

01

Plan the grasped assembly

RRT-Connect searches with the object and both carrier footprints. Grasp offsets change the orientations that can fit through an aperture.

02

Track object motion

A 10 Hz object-level MPC follows measured path progress. A stall does not advance the reference simply because time passes.

03

Act locally

The same frozen actor runs at 50 Hz for each robot. Local observations and references produce 21 action outputs per robot.

Shared in the evaluated system

Assembly geometry, object motion references, and supervised phase/height decisions. Attachment state informs progression.

Local to each robot

Its observation, action inference and motor commands. Shared weights do not imply identical actions from different states.

What is actually communicated

Object state, motion references, and supervised phase/height are shared. Joint and hand feedback remain local. These simulator interfaces are not measured network bandwidth.

Planning, timing, and stage-level diagnostics

Grasps change what fits.

The illustrated beam’s admissible yaw narrows from 25.8° to 5.5° when the carrier footprints are included. Planning only for the payload misses this constraint.

Global and local geometry must agree.

Aligning the collision representation and margin raises the paired result from 36/40 to 40/40. This changes both geometry and margin; it does not isolate either effect.

A late partner need not prevent acquisition.

With a 7.68 s activation delay, attachment occurs at 1.88 and 10.76 s; transport begins at 13.44 s. A supervisor controls progression in this experiment.

Failures concentrate before transport.

All five failed missions stop before verified-route transport: two panel tips, a beam timeout, an incomplete office lift and a living-room search failure.

Why training is changing: finish imitation, then learn from rewards

The initial demonstrations and corrective fitting rounds are complete. An audit found that the first PPO run still applied a teacher action-regression loss with initial coefficient 10 and decay 0.998 per update. At update 296 its coefficient remained about 5.54. This auxiliary loss was separate from the environment reward; it still constrained policy learning.

The new continuation explicitly sets teacher loss and policy-anchor coefficients to zero. The actor samples every action and learns from task-return advantages; the centralized critic predicts simulated returns. Actor, critic, optimizer, exploration state and observation normalization resume from a saved PPO checkpoint. Repeated training chunks retain that critic. The next motion curriculum also starts with reward-only PPO, without a new demonstration fit.

Use “Critic learning” for value loss and explained variance, and “Teacher influence” for the imitation coefficient and PPO policy loss. Lower value loss alone does not establish better control: independent success, drops and contact safety remain the acceptance measures. Rewards, curriculum and current problems

Acquisition credit requires sustained opposing finger contacts. Firmness credit saturates; slip, excessive force, premature closure, hand-frame error, finger mechanical work and sustained torque are penalized. Supported object progress and the final requested pose earn task reward. Firmness is a contact-quality proxy, not a proof of force closure; mechanical work in joules is separate from electrical energy.

The hand-target acquisition profiles currently sample sliding friction 3.0–4.0. A broader 2.5–3.5 range and a 40-episode, 90%-success promotion rule are configurable; contact-curriculum promotion is disabled in this initial acquisition comparison. Changes in material difficulty apply at reset. The final frozen evaluation must exceed 90% separately on complete transport, rotation, obstacle and recovery cohorts. Lift-only clips, fixture tests and training curves do not satisfy that test.

Earlier 1.5 kg development trials used 30 mm handles with additional hand clearance. A scripted controller now acquires four physical grasps, lifts 6 cm and holds for 8 seconds. A timestep check at 500 and 1000 Hz changes the object trajectory by at most 1.25 mm and 0.16°. These are short physics diagnostics. Both V2 and V3 learned actors passed 0/20 fresh grasp-and-lift trials, despite successful teacher demonstrations. The distance-focused PPO reward variant then acquired four-hand support in 18/20 paired trials, but completed 0/20 lift-and-hold tasks: 17 stopped on unintended object–robot contact, two on self-contact and one on unsupported travel. The matched variant with an additional secure-contact cost acquired four-hand support in 0/20 trials. Corrective geometric action targets were also tested during PPO; they did not yet produce successful frozen-policy lift-and-hold trials. Neither contact acquisition nor teacher-assisted collection counts as learned task success.

Superseded, 10 September 2026. Thirty frozen cohorts of that formulation scored zero in 566 trials. The cause was measured and it was geometric, not a reward problem: the grasp reference the policy was told to track was a pose its own closed hand cannot occupy — placing the measured closed hand at the assigned palm frame overlaps the carry rail by 19–20 mm, and the alignment kernel therefore paid a mean of 0.009 across whole training runs. The earlier declared test completes 46/48 full missions across twelve scenes, with 1–2 kg loads, ±5% dimensions, 0.08–0.50 m approach radii and activation delays up to eight seconds. It uses a gated controller and idealized welded retention. It does not establish recovery with a loose or dropped grasp.

One policy, local information
The current experiments compare a small causal transformer and a feedforward MLP over a separate sensor history per robot. It receives own encoders, grasp errors, local load/contact, relative object SE(3) pose, task geometry and measurement ages. Partner state and coordination-phase inputs are absent. Each run records its architecture and history settings.
The planner chooses the pose
Desired object orientation and nearby path targets come from the high-level planner. The spatial reference follows measured progress. The actor commands base motion, lift/arm/torso targets and gripper closure. Each run records its motor-command convention; a zero lift rate holds position.
Contact and uncertainty
Finite, breakable contact attachments require physical rail contact and closure. Object, hand and odometry estimates include configurable noise, latency and dropout. A 2° base-tilt guard remains strict. Contact forces are an uncalibrated simulation approximation.


Open problems and research basis
Recovering from a mid-run release, arbitrary approach offsets, and learned large-angle object flips remain open. Regrasp alone is insufficient: a fixed-command diagnostic also reconnects but fails the full task. Memory and load-feedback ablations, broader scene tests and hardware calibration remain necessary.
The temporal policy follows the causal-transformer direction of Radosavovic et al. Decentralized shared-load coordination is informed by decPLM and decMBC. These precedents do not establish reliability for our robot or grasp mechanics.

Reading the comparison and research-group map
“No runtime messages” describes explicit inter-robot communication, not absence of a common task command or physical/visual coupling. “Shared task state” and “shared supervisor” are separate dependencies. Human-to-robot teaching is not the same as human–robot teaming.
Stanford connections include Bohg’s IPRL, Finn’s IRIS, Liu’s TML and Sadigh’s ILIAD. ETH’s CRL/RSL connects Coros and Hutter; the payload-aware planner is CRL work. Oregon State’s papers are by Pandit and colleagues with Fern; Fern co-directs the Dynamic Robotics Lab with Hurst, who is not an author of these two papers. Berkeley RAIL (Levine) and ICON (Mehr) are distinct groups. SAI’s revised paper lists Sydney, Zhejiang University and Zhejiang University of Technology; Zhi’s previous CMU appointment is not the paper’s affiliation. CLAS lists Volkswagen’s Machine Learning Research Lab.
