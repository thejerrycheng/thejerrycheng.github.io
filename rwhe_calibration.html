Certifiably Correct Hand-Eye Calibration

Where is the camera on the robot, and where is the target in the world? Every measurement closes one loop, AiX = YBi, and the problem is not convex: a local solver from a bad guess lands in a wrong answer that looks fine. The IJRR paper relaxes the rotations into a semidefinite program that returns the global optimum together with a certificate, for any number of sensors and targets and even for cameras that do not know their own scale. It was re-implemented in Python, its simulations re-run, and its real eight-camera rig solved again from the published measurements.

Fifteen seconds: a camera on a robot hand orbits a target, twenty-four local solves from random starts scatter into local minima, and the convex relaxation lands on the one certified answer. The full demo adds the simulation tables and the real rig.

In one strip

1 · Loop

Ai X = Y Bi

The arm reports the hand pose Ai; the camera reports the target pose Bi. The unknowns are hand→camera X and base→target Y. With several cameras and several targets it is a bipartite graph of such loops, and a monocular camera adds an unknown scale α.

2 · Relax

Rotations only, then lift

Translations and α are eliminated by a Schur complement. Each rotation becomes nine numbers with 21 quadratic constraints; the Shor relaxation is a semidefinite program whose value bounds the true cost from below. If the rounded answer meets the bound, it is provably optimal.

3 · Certify

Eight cameras, sixteen tags, one problem

The published rig data: 73 camera–tag pairs, 3,230 pose pairs. One 217-dimensional SDP returns all 24 poses with a duality gap at machine precision, and with the scale free it reports the tags 2.5 % smaller than measured, as the paper found.

Method

The maximum-likelihood cost is a sum over every loop of a translation term and a chordal rotation term, both quadratic in the unknowns once the rotation matrices are vectorised:

J = ½ Σ ( 1/σ² ‖ RA tX + α tA − tY − RY tB ‖² + κ ‖ RA RX − RY RB ‖F² )

Translation noise is Gaussian (σ) and rotation noise is Langevin on SO(3) (concentration κ; 125 ≈ 3.6°, 12 ≈ 12°). The translations and the scale appear without constraints, so they are solved out by a generalized Schur complement, leaving a quadratic form in the rotations alone. Each rotation is kept in SO(3) by 21 homogenised quadratic constraints (row and column orthonormality plus the right-hand rule), and the relaxation drops the rank-one condition on the lifted variable.

  • Solver: the primal SDP in cvxpy, SCS at 10⁻¹¹ with a Clarabel fallback; leading-eigenvector rounding, per-block SO(3) projection, translations and α back-substituted.
  • Certificate: ρ̂ = (p − d)/|d| between the rounded cost and the SDP bound, and the eigenvalue ratio of the lifted matrix.
  • Identifiability: a single sensor–target pair excited about two distinct rotation axes makes the whole weakly connected graph identifiable (paper Theorem 3 / Corollary 4); the re-run checks this criterion on every edge of the real data.
  • Baselines: a linear two-stage solver in the Shah / Wang style and a local Levenberg–Marquardt solver on the same objective, started at random, from the linear solution, or (unknown scale) 10° and 10 cm from the truth.

The real rig

The public repository ships the processed measurements of the paper's experiment. With the paper's weights (κ = 125, σ = 0.1 m) the joint problem has 24 rotations, a 217×217 lifted matrix and 504 constraints. SCS solves it in 230 s with the scale known and 81 s with the scale free.

What the re-run found

The paper's claims held; the notes below are about getting there from scratch.

1

The certificate is only as good as the solver's tolerance

An interior-point solver at 10⁻¹⁰ returned duality gaps of −1.5e-6: the rounded cost was below the bound, which cannot happen exactly and simply meant the bound was wrong in the sixth digit. The first-order SCS solver at 10⁻¹¹ gives −2.5e-9 on the same problem, the paper's floor. A "negative gap" is the sign to tighten the solver, not to celebrate.

2

The random-start local solver is the whole argument

On identical data and the identical objective, Levenberg–Marquardt from random poses finished in a local minimum in 1–40 % of the runs, with hand–camera errors of hundreds of millimetres and no warning sign in the residual. From the linear solution it always reached the optimum. The SDP reached it from nothing, every time, with the gap to prove it.

3

Column-major matters, and so does where s goes

Two bugs cost an evening: vec(AXB) = (Bᵀ ⊗ A) vec(X) only in column-major order, and in the known-scale case the homogenising variable s must stay in the reduced problem (it couples to the translation of A) and must be the last entry the constraints refer to. Both are documented in the code so nobody re-derives them.

Reproduce

Python only (numpy, scipy, cvxpy with SCS and Clarabel, matplotlib). The real-world CSVs come from the authors' public repository. Simulation campaigns run as shards because macOS process pools and SDP solvers do not mix.

cd experiments for k in 0 1 2 3 4; do python sim_studies.py 100 --shard $k --nshards 5 & done; wait python sim_studies.py # merge → tables + figures python real_data.py # the 8-camera rig, ~5 min python make_tables.py && (cd ../docs && make) # technical report python make_video.py
