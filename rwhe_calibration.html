Certifiably Correct Hand-Eye Calibration

Where is the camera on the robot, and where is the target in the world? Every measurement closes one loop, AiX = YBi, and the problem is not convex: a local solver from a bad guess lands in a wrong answer that looks fine. The IJRR paper relaxes the rotations into a semidefinite program that returns the global optimum together with a certificate, for any number of sensors and targets and even for cameras that do not know their own scale. It was re-implemented in Python, its simulations re-run, and its real eight-camera rig solved again from the published measurements.

Fifteen seconds: a camera on a robot hand orbits a target, twenty-four local solves from random starts scatter into local minima, and the convex relaxation lands on the one certified answer. The full demo adds the simulation tables and the real rig.

The problem, in fifteen seconds

What is measured

  • Ai: the pose of the hand (or of the rig, from motion capture) at every time i.
  • Bi: the pose of a fiducial target as the camera sees it, from an AprilTag detector with a hand-measured tag size.
  • Which camera saw which tag: a bipartite graph. On the real rig, 16 tags, 8 cameras, 73 edges, 3,230 pose pairs.

What we want

  • Xj: where each tag sits in the world.
  • Yk: where each camera sits on the hand. That is the calibration.
  • α: the true tag scale, when the hand-measured size cannot be trusted (the monocular case).

The insight

  • Every observation closes a loop: AiXj = YkBi.
  • The rotations make the least-squares fit non-convex, so a local solver can stop in the wrong valley without warning.
  • Lift the rotations into a positive-semidefinite matrix, drop the rank-one condition, and the relaxed problem is convex: no initial guess, and a duality gap that proves the answer is the global optimum.

In one strip

1 · Loop

Ai X = Y Bi

The arm reports the hand pose Ai; the camera reports the target pose Bi. The unknowns are hand→camera X and base→target Y. With several cameras and several targets it is a bipartite graph of such loops, and a monocular camera adds an unknown scale α.

2 · Relax

Rotations only, then lift

Translations and α are eliminated by a Schur complement. Each rotation becomes nine numbers with 21 quadratic constraints; the Shor relaxation is a semidefinite program whose value bounds the true cost from below. If the rounded answer meets the bound, it is provably optimal.

3 · Certify

Eight cameras, sixteen tags, one problem

The published rig data: 73 camera–tag pairs, 3,230 pose pairs. One 217-dimensional SDP returns all 24 poses with a duality gap at machine precision, and with the scale free it reports the tags 2.5 % smaller than measured, as the paper found.

Method

The maximum-likelihood cost is a sum over every loop of a translation term and a chordal rotation term, both quadratic in the unknowns once the rotation matrices are vectorised:

J = ½ Σ ( 1/σ² ‖ RA tX + α tA − tY − RY tB ‖² + κ ‖ RA RX − RY RB ‖F² )

The translations and the scale appear without constraints, so they are solved out by a generalized Schur complement, leaving a quadratic form in the rotations alone. Each rotation is kept in SO(3) by 21 homogenised quadratic constraints (row and column orthonormality plus the right-hand rule), and the relaxation drops the rank-one condition on the lifted variable.

  • Noise model (paper §8): translations get Gaussian noise N(0, σ²I); rotations get isotropic Langevin noise, p(R) ∝ exp(κ tr(R̄ᵀR)), sampled by rejection. κ = 125 is about 3.6° of angular error, κ = 12 about 12°.
  • Identifiability (Theorem 3, Corollary 4): on a weakly connected graph, one sensor–target edge with three measurements whose relative rotations have distinct principal axes fixes that edge, and every other X and Y follows by propagating loop closures across the graph. The re-run checks this criterion on each edge of the real data.
  • Tightness (Proposition 7): for noise-free data with a unique solution the relaxation is exact, and it stays tight under moderate noise. The certificate is ρ̂ = (p − d)/|d| between the rounded cost and the SDP bound, plus the eigenvalue ratio λ₂/λ₁ of the lifted matrix.
  • Solver: the primal SDP in cvxpy, SCS at 10⁻¹¹ with a Clarabel fallback; leading-eigenvector rounding, per-block SO(3) projection, translations and α back-substituted.
  • Baselines: a linear two-stage solver in the Shah / Wang style and a local Levenberg–Marquardt solver on the same objective, started at random, from the linear solution, or (unknown scale) 10° and 10 cm from the truth.

Simulation studies

Setup

The setup: what is given, what is estimated

A camera on a robot hand visits poses on a sphere around a fiducial target; the ledger of knowns, unknowns and ground truth appears alongside the measurements as they are taken.

Recording

The campaign, recorded

Four noise levels, one hundred seeded runs each, four solvers per run. Every random-start local solve lands on the error axis as it finishes; the certified SDP points stack at the bottom.

Given

  • Hand poses Ai with translation noise σ ∈ {{1, 5}} cm and Langevin rotation noise κ ∈ {{125, 12}}.
  • Target poses Bi with the same noise; in the monocular study their translations are scaled by α = 0.5.
  • Sphere study: 100 poses on a sphere around one target. Four-camera study: 108 poses, four fixed cameras watching a target on the hand.

Estimated

  • X (hand–camera, or the target on the hand) and Y (world–base, or each of the four cameras).
  • α in the monocular study.
  • Four solvers on identical data: linear two-stage, local LM from random starts, local LM from the linear solution, and the certified SDP.

Ground truth and protocol

  • Random rigid transforms per run, fixed seeds, 100 runs per noise level: 1,600 SDP solves plus baselines.
  • Errors: translation of X and Y in mm, chordal rotation error in degrees, scale error in percent.
  • The certificate is recorded for every solve: relative gap, eigenvalue ratio, solver time.

The certificate as a picture: the spectrum of the lifted matrix

All 217 eigenvalues of the SDP solution Z. A tight relaxation has exactly one non-zero eigenvalue: the rest sit at solver precision, twelve orders of magnitude below, which is what lets the rank-one factor be read off as the certified rotations.

Paper Figs. 7–8: the environment seen from camera 0, and the rig. Eight hardware-synchronised Point Grey Blackfly S cameras, OptiTrack markers and a VectorNav VN-100. The tag at the bottom left of the middle image carries its own markers, so its world pose is ground truth.

Pipeline, as in the paper’s §9.1: OptiTrack gives the rig pose A, an AprilTag detector gives each camera-to-tag pose B, the two clocks are joined by interpolating OptiTrack onto the image timestamps, and a RANSAC on the loop closure Xj = A⁻¹YkB (inliers within 0.6 m and 60°) rejects bad detections before the batch solve.

The public repository ships the processed measurements. With the paper’s weights (κ = 125, σ = 0.1 m) the joint problem has 24 rotations, a 217×217 lifted matrix and 504 constraints. SCS solves it in 230 s with the scale known and 81 s with the scale free.

What the re-run found

The paper's claims held; the notes below are about getting there from scratch.

1

The certificate is only as good as the solver's tolerance

An interior-point solver at 10⁻¹⁰ returned duality gaps of −1.5e-6: the rounded cost was below the bound, which cannot happen exactly and simply meant the bound was wrong in the sixth digit. The first-order SCS solver at 10⁻¹¹ gives −2.5e-9 on the same problem, the paper's floor. A "negative gap" is the sign to tighten the solver, not to celebrate.

2

The random-start local solver is the whole argument

On identical data and the identical objective, Levenberg–Marquardt from random poses finished in a local minimum in 1–40 % of the runs, with hand–camera errors of hundreds of millimetres and no warning sign in the residual. From the linear solution it always reached the optimum. The SDP reached it from nothing, every time, with the gap to prove it.

3

Column-major matters, and so does where s goes

Two bugs cost an evening: vec(AXB) = (Bᵀ ⊗ A) vec(X) only in column-major order, and in the known-scale case the homogenising variable s must stay in the reduced problem (it couples to the translation of A) and must be the last entry the constraints refer to. Both are documented in the code so nobody re-derives them.

Reproduce

Python only (numpy, scipy, cvxpy with SCS and Clarabel, matplotlib). The real-world CSVs come from the authors' public repository. Simulation campaigns run as shards because macOS process pools and SDP solvers do not mix.

cd experiments for k in 0 1 2 3 4; do python sim_studies.py 100 --shard $k --nshards 5 & done; wait python sim_studies.py # merge → tables + figures python real_data.py # the 8-camera rig, ~5 min python make_tables.py && (cd ../docs && make) # technical report python make_video.py
