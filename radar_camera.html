Radar-Camera Spatiotemporal Calibration

A 3D radar measures its own velocity from Doppler; a monocular camera measures where it is, up to scale, from what it sees. Put both on one continuous-time trajectory and the relative pose between them, the camera's scale and the offset between the two clocks all fall out, with no reflectors and no overlapping fields of view. The T-RO paper was re-implemented from scratch in Python: B-splines on rotations, the joint estimator, the paper's simulation studies, its identifiability analysis, and the radar front end on the original 2020 handheld recordings.

Fifteen seconds: the rig flies its B-spline in front of a checkerboard while both data streams arrive on their own clocks, the radar stream is slid in time until it matches the camera-derived motion, and the extrinsic estimate converges. The full demo adds the simulation studies, the identifiability check and the real corridor data.

In one strip

1 · Two streams

One body, two clocks, two frames

Camera poses at 30 Hz, up to a scale α. Radar ego-velocities at 20 Hz, stamped τ late. The trajectory is a cumulative cubic B-spline on R³ × SO(3), so it can be queried at any instant and differentiated analytically.

2 · Align

τ is just another parameter

hj = Rcrᵀ ( Rwcᵀ ṗ + ω × r ) evaluated at tj + τ. A linear closed form on the paper's identifiability model gives Rcr, r, α and a τ grid search; sparse Levenberg–Marquardt over spline and parameters finishes the job.

3 · Real data

The 2020 handheld recordings

TI AWR1843 radar, Point Grey Flea3, VectorNav IMU, walked through the corridors of FER Zagreb. The RANSAC front end validates 80 % of the scans and measures the radar noise the paper only assumed.

Method

Temporal calibration is natural in continuous time: shifting one sensor's timestamps means querying the trajectory somewhere else, so the trajectory must be a function, not a list. The paper uses the cumulative B-spline of Sommer et al., R(u) = Ri ∏ exp(λj(u) log(Ri+j−1ᵀRi+j)), whose body angular velocity has a closed form; the re-implementation verifies it against finite differences to 10⁻⁹.

er = log( R̃kᵀ Rwc(tk) ), et = p̃k − α pc(tk), ev = hj − Rcrᵀ( Rwcᵀ ṗ + ω × r )|t_j + τ

Unknowns: about 300 translation and 300 rotation control points for a 30-s run, plus Rcr, r, α and τ. The Jacobian is sparse (each residual touches four control points), so finite differences through the sparsity pattern need only 57 evaluations, and the normal equations are solved by sparse LU. Rotation control points are perturbed locally and re-linearised after every accepted step.

  • Initialisation: the paper's identifiability model a = α Rcr h(t + τ) − α ω × r is linear in vec(αRcr) and αr for a fixed τ; solve it on a τ grid, take α from the singular values and Rcr from the SO(3) projection.
  • Camera noise as in the paper: a checkerboard is projected, pixel noise added, and solvePnP run with the wrong square size, which is exactly what produces a scaled position with realistic pose noise.
  • Identifiability: the stacked gradient matrix of the paper's eq. 23 has rank 7 of 8 when the body rotates about one axis only, and is zero at rest.

The handheld rig

The paper's handheld experiment was recorded in 2020 at the University of Zagreb: a TI AWR1843BOOST radar, a Point Grey Flea3 camera and a VectorNav VN-100, carried through office corridors. The archived bags hold radar clouds with Doppler at 10 Hz, 1920×1200 images at 10 Hz and the IMU at 100 Hz. Every header stamp is zero, so the bag receive times are the clock.

The 3-point RANSAC ego-velocity estimator (the paper's eqs. 7–9) validates 476 of 594 scans with 21 of 23 returns as inliers, an inlier range-rate spread of 0.031 m/s and a median least-squares velocity uncertainty of 0.054 m/s. The rig walks at 1.5 m/s and turns at 0.5 rad/s: closer to trajectory B than A.

What could not be re-run: the joint spatiotemporal solve. It needs the monocular camera trajectory from ORB-SLAM3 and the camera intrinsics, and neither is available here; the AprilGrid in the calibration bag is not detected by two AprilTag libraries at this resolution. The paper's real-data tables are therefore not reproduced, and the page says so.

What the re-run found

Two of the paper's statements were tested harder than the paper could, and one part could not be tested at all.

1

Excitation, not noise, decides translation and offset

Across the four noise levels the rotation and scale errors barely move on either trajectory. Translation and offset errors on trajectory A are several times those on B at every noise level, and their reported 1σ under-states the spread there. The lever-arm term ω × r is the only thing that ties the radar position to the camera, so a calibration drive needs to rotate about two axes, fast.

2

A closed-form start exists, and it is good enough

The paper's identifiability model, meant for a proof, doubles as an initialiser: linear in the scaled rotation and scaled lever arm, with a one-dimensional search in τ. Median start error 1.3° on B, and the joint estimator converged from it in 62 % of the runs without touching the ground truth.

3

The real radar is quieter than the paper assumed

The paper places the ego-velocity noise between 0.05 and 0.15 m/s and notes it is "closer to the lower end". The archived corridor run gives 0.031 m/s on the inliers and 0.054 m/s as the propagated velocity uncertainty, so the low-noise simulation rows are the ones that describe this hardware.

Reproduce

Python only (numpy, scipy, OpenCV for PnP, matplotlib, the pure-Python rosbags reader). The simulation campaign is sharded; a 30-s solve takes 5–10 s.

cd experiments for k in 0 1 2 3 4; do python sim_studies.py 100 --shard $k --nshards 5 & done; wait python sim_studies.py # merge → histograms, identifiability python real_frontend.py path/to/handheld.bag # radar front end on the real rig python make_tables.py && (cd ../docs && make) # technical report python make_video.py
