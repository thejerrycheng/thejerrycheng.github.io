Radar-Camera Spatiotemporal Calibration

A 3D radar measures its own velocity from Doppler; a monocular camera measures where it is, up to scale, from what it sees. Put both on one continuous-time trajectory and the relative pose between them, the camera's scale and the offset between the two clocks all fall out, with no reflectors and no overlapping fields of view. The T-RO paper was re-implemented from scratch in Python: B-splines on rotations, the joint estimator, the paper's simulation studies, its identifiability analysis, and the radar front end on the original 2020 handheld recordings.

Fifteen seconds: the rig flies its B-spline in front of a checkerboard while both data streams arrive on their own clocks, the radar stream is slid in time until it matches the camera-derived motion, and the extrinsic estimate converges. The full demo adds the simulation studies, the identifiability check and the real corridor data.

The problem, in fifteen seconds

What is measured

  • Radar: one 3-D ego-velocity per scan, from the Doppler of the static returns (a 3-point RANSAC), on the radar’s clock.
  • Camera: its own trajectory from monocular SLAM or from a target, correct only up to a scale α, on the camera’s clock.
  • No shared field of view, no radar reflector, no checkerboard in the calibration run.

What we want

  • Rcr and r: the rotation and lever arm between radar and camera.
  • τ: the clock offset between the two sensors.
  • α: the scale of the camera trajectory.

The insight

  • Both sensors ride one rigid body, so the radar velocity must equal the camera velocity rotated into the radar frame plus the lever-arm term ω × r, evaluated at t + τ.
  • A continuous-time B-spline makes the camera trajectory a smooth function of time, so velocity, angular velocity and the shift τ all come from the same curve.
  • One sparse least-squares problem then returns all four unknowns, with a covariance, and a rank test says whether the motion was rich enough.

In one strip

1 · Two streams

One body, two clocks, two frames

Camera poses at 30 Hz, up to a scale α. Radar ego-velocities at 20 Hz, stamped τ late. The trajectory is a cumulative cubic B-spline on R³ × SO(3), so it can be queried at any instant and differentiated analytically.

2 · Align

τ is just another parameter

hj = Rcrᵀ ( Rwcᵀ ṗ + ω × r ) evaluated at tj + τ. A linear closed form on the paper's identifiability model gives Rcr, r, α and a τ grid search; sparse Levenberg–Marquardt over spline and parameters finishes the job.

3 · Real data

The 2020 handheld recordings

TI AWR1843 radar, Point Grey Flea3, VectorNav IMU, walked through the corridors of FER Zagreb. The RANSAC front end validates 80 % of the scans and measures the radar noise the paper only assumed.

Watch it work

Three short loops from the re-implementation: the continuous-time trajectory, the measurement model that ties the two sensors together, and the motions that break it.

1 · Represent

Continuous time: a cumulative B-spline

Control points every 0.1 s on R³ × SO(3); the curve, its velocity and angular velocity are all closed-form, which is what lets τ be a differentiable unknown. Paper Fig. 10 shows the same thing on the real rig.

2 · Constrain

The measurement model

The camera’s spline velocity, the lever-arm term ω × r, and the radar’s ego-velocity h, sampled τ later. Slide the radar stream in time and the residual collapses at the true offset.

3 · Identifiability

When it cannot work

The singular values of the paper’s observability matrix along the flight: rotation about a single axis drops one, standing still drops all eight.

Method

Temporal calibration is natural in continuous time: shifting one sensor’s timestamps means querying the trajectory somewhere else, so the trajectory must be a function, not a list. The paper uses the cumulative B-spline of Sommer et al., R(u) = Ri ∏ exp(λj(u) log(Ri+j−1ᵀRi+j)), whose body angular velocity has a closed form; the re-implementation verifies it against finite differences to 10⁻⁹.

er = log( R̃kᵀ Rwc(tk) ), et = p̃k − α pc(tk), ev = hj − Rcrᵀ( Rwcᵀ ṗ + ω × r )|t_j + τ

Unknowns: about 300 translation and 300 rotation control points for a 30-s run, plus Rcr, r, α and τ. The Jacobian is sparse (each residual touches four control points), so finite differences through the sparsity pattern need only 57 evaluations, and the normal equations are solved by sparse LU. Rotation control points are perturbed locally and re-linearised after every accepted step.

  • Radar front end (paper eqs. 7–9): a static return at direction u has range-rate −u·h; three returns fix h, RANSAC removes movers and multipath, least squares on the inliers gives h and its covariance.
  • Initialisation: the paper’s identifiability model a = α Rcr h(t + τ) − α ω × r is linear in vec(αRcr) and αr for a fixed τ; solve it on a τ grid, take α from the singular values and Rcr from the SO(3) projection.
  • Camera noise as in the paper: a checkerboard is projected, pixel noise added, and solvePnP run with the wrong square size, which is exactly what produces a scaled position with realistic pose noise.
  • Identifiability (paper §IV, Appendix A): the observability rank condition on the Lie derivatives of the measurement holds when the body rotates about two or more axes. Rotation about a single axis and standing still are the degenerate motions; with τ known, spatial calibration alone is locally weakly observable (the appendix’s quaternion analysis).

Simulation studies

Setup

The setup: what is given, what is estimated

The rig flies trajectory B in front of the checkerboard; the radar returns, the PnP camera poses on their own clock, and the ledger of knowns, unknowns and ground truth for every trial.

Recording

The campaign, recorded

Two trajectories, four noise levels, one hundred seeded trials each. Every finished solve drops its four errors onto the histograms as the campaign runs.

Given

  • Radar ego-velocities at 20 Hz with noise σr ∈ {{0.05, 0.15}} m/s.
  • Camera poses at 30 Hz from PnP on a 9×7 checkerboard with corner noise σc ∈ {{0.2, 0.4}} px and the wrong square size.
  • 30-s runs on trajectory A (fast, little rotation) or B (slow, twisting).

Estimated

  • Rcr, r, α, τ.
  • About 600 spline control points for the camera trajectory.
  • Started from the closed-form initialiser, never from the truth.

Ground truth

  • Random extrinsics per trial; α = 0.5; τ = 50 ms.
  • 100 seeded trials per setting: 800 solves, 5–25 s each.

The handheld rig

The paper’s 2020 corridor recording from the University of Zagreb, replayed from the archived bag: the camera frames, the radar returns with the RANSAC inliers, the three ego-velocity components, and the gyro, all on the bag clock.

Paper Figs. 7–9: the rig (TI AWR1843BOOST radar, Point Grey Flea3, VectorNav VN-100), the verification target (a trihedral corner reflector with an AprilTag on radar-transparent cardboard in front of it), and the office where the targetless part of the run was recorded.

The archived bags hold radar clouds with Doppler at 10 Hz, 1920×1200 images at 10 Hz and the IMU at 100 Hz. Every header stamp is zero, so the bag receive times are the clock.

The 3-point RANSAC ego-velocity estimator validates 476 of 594 scans with 21 of 23 returns as inliers, an inlier range-rate spread of 0.031 m/s and a median least-squares velocity uncertainty of 0.054 m/s. The rig walks at 1.5 m/s and turns at 0.5 rad/s: closer to trajectory B than A.

What could not be re-run: the joint spatiotemporal solve. It needs the monocular camera trajectory from ORB-SLAM3 and the camera intrinsics, and neither is available here; the AprilGrid in the calibration bag is not detected by two AprilTag libraries at this resolution. The paper’s real-data tables below are therefore the paper’s own numbers, reproduced for reference and labelled as such.

Where calibration can be done (paper §VI-E).

At least four stationary landmarks in the radar’s view, more being better; enough light and texture for the camera’s SLAM; no crowds of moving targets, dim scenes, or fog. The vehicle problem is poorly conditioned because a car barely rolls or pitches, so the smallest eigenvalue of the identifiability matrix sits near zero and the estimate is sensitive to noise.

What the re-run found

Two of the paper's statements were tested harder than the paper could, and one part could not be tested at all.

1

Excitation, not noise, decides translation and offset

Across the four noise levels the rotation and scale errors barely move on either trajectory. Translation and offset errors on trajectory A are several times those on B at every noise level, and their reported 1σ under-states the spread there. The lever-arm term ω × r is the only thing that ties the radar position to the camera, so a calibration drive needs to rotate about two axes, fast.

2

A closed-form start exists, and it is good enough

The paper's identifiability model, meant for a proof, doubles as an initialiser: linear in the scaled rotation and scaled lever arm, with a one-dimensional search in τ. Median start error 1.3° on B, and the joint estimator converged from it in 62 % of the runs without touching the ground truth.

3

The real radar is quieter than the paper assumed

The paper places the ego-velocity noise between 0.05 and 0.15 m/s and notes it is "closer to the lower end". The archived corridor run gives 0.031 m/s on the inliers and 0.054 m/s as the propagated velocity uncertainty, so the low-noise simulation rows are the ones that describe this hardware.

Reproduce

Python only (numpy, scipy, OpenCV for PnP, matplotlib, the pure-Python rosbags reader). The simulation campaign is sharded; a 30-s solve takes 5–10 s.

cd experiments for k in 0 1 2 3 4; do python sim_studies.py 100 --shard $k --nshards 5 & done; wait python sim_studies.py # merge → histograms, identifiability python real_frontend.py path/to/handheld.bag # radar front end on the real rig python make_tables.py && (cd ../docs && make) # technical report python make_video.py
